Quote sources and credits

These eight excerpts are bundled with Stoic Block. Each was compared with the historical edition identified below on November 20, 2026. Links to scans and rights references open only when selected and require an internet connection.

Marcus Aurelius · translated by George Long

The Thoughts of the Emperor M. Aurelius Antoninus, London: Bell and Daldy, 1862. The translation was published before 1931 and is public domain in the United States. Line breaks and spacing are normalized.

  1. Meditations 8.22: “Attend to the matter which is before thee, whether it is an opinion or an act or a word.” Printed page 129, scan page 211. The following paragraph is a self-reproach about postponing becoming good.
  2. Meditations 4.2: “Let no act be done without a purpose, nor otherwise than according to the perfect principles of art.” Printed page 40, scan page 122. This is the complete numbered section.
  3. Meditations 6.3: “Look within. Let neither the peculiar quality of anything nor its value escape thee.” Printed page 80, scan page 162. This is the complete numbered section.

Epictetus · translated by Elizabeth Carter

All the Works of Epictetus, Dublin: Hulton Bradley, 1759. The translation was published before 1931 and is public domain in the United States. Line breaks and spacing are normalized, and the historical long-s glyph is represented as “s”; historical capitalization and punctuation are retained.

  1. Enchiridion 8: “Require not Things to happen as you wish; but wish them to happen as they do happen; and you will go on well.” Printed page 391, scan page 443. This is the complete numbered section.
  2. Enchiridion 12: “Begin therefore from little Things.” Printed page 392, scan page 444. This sentence introduces examples of practicing composure in response to small losses.

Seneca · translated by Richard M. Gummere

Ad Lucilium Epistulae Morales, volume I, London: William Heinemann; New York: G. P. Putnam’s Sons, 1917. The translation was published before 1931 and is public domain in the United States. Line breaks and spacing are normalized; apostrophe style is normalized.

  1. Moral Letters to Lucilius 1.2: “Lay hold of to-day’s task, and you will not need to depend so much upon to-morrow’s.” Printed page 3, scan page 32. The letter discusses time, mortality, and postponement.
  2. Moral Letters to Lucilius 2.3: “There is nothing so efficacious that it can be helpful while it is being shifted about.” Printed page 7, scan page 36. Seneca describes reading deeply rather than continually switching authors, following examples of medicine and plants.
  3. Moral Letters to Lucilius 6.4: “No good thing is pleasant to possess, without friends to share it.” Printed page 27, scan page 56. Seneca discusses sharing knowledge and friendship.
